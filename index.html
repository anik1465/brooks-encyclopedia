01

Core ideas

Six ideas carry the whole method. Everything else in this encyclopedia is these ideas applied to specific chart situations.

price action

Everything you can learn from price movement alone: the bars, their opens/closes/tails, and where they sit relative to earlier bars. Brooks trades from one 5-minute chart with a single 20-bar EMA and nothing else — no indicators, no news, no volume. Price action is, in his words, the inescapable footprint of what institutions are doing.

the market cycle spike → channel → trading range → breakout

Markets endlessly repeat one cycle: a trading range breaks out into a spike (strong trend move), the spike slows into a channel (weaker trend with pullbacks), the channel flattens into a trading range, and eventually the range breaks out and starts the cycle again — on every timeframe.

Each phase demands different tactics. The #1 novice error is using trend tactics in a range, or range tactics in a trend.

inertia the two 80% rules

Markets strongly tend to keep doing what they're already doing. In a trend, roughly 80% of reversal attempts fail and become flags. In a trading range, roughly 80% of breakout attempts fail and reverse back inside.

These two numbers alone tell you which side of most trades to be on.

always in

Ask: "If I were forced to hold a position right now, long or short, which would I pick?" That answer is the always-in direction — the market's current trend as traders actually experience it. Flipping it usually requires at least two consecutive strong trend bars in the new direction with follow-through. While the market is always-in long, treat every sell setup as a probable failure and only take longs.

trader's equation

A trade is worth taking only if probability of winning × reward is greater than probability of losing × risk. The three variables always trade off — the market never offers high probability, big reward, and small risk at once, because someone smart is on the other side. Good with-trend pullback entries are roughly 60% trades; strong reversal entries may be 40% but pay 2–3× the risk.

edge

A setup with a positive trader's equation — a real mathematical advantage. Brooks stresses that edges are always small and fleeting; if a big obvious edge existed, institutions would trade it away instantly.

directional probability

The chance the market moves X points up before it moves X points down. Most of the time it hovers near 50–50; in a clear, strong trend it tilts to roughly 60–40 in the trend's favor — which is why with-trend trading is the default.

second entry second signal favorite

A setup that triggers, fails, and then sets up again in the same direction. The second attempt is roughly twice as reliable as the first, because the failed first attempt trapped traders whose exits now fuel your trade. Second entries at the top or bottom of a trading range are among Brooks' favorite trades.

fractal

Every pattern repeats on every timeframe. A single bar on a 15-minute chart contains a whole day's worth of structure on the 1-minute chart; a trading range on the 5-minute chart is just a pullback on the 60-minute chart. The reading skills are identical everywhere.

institutions & HFT

Banks, funds, and high-frequency trading firms generate the overwhelming majority of volume, so every pattern you see is created by them. You can't move the market — your job is to spot what they're doing and tag along. Since an institution is on both sides of every tick, never be certain of anything.

strong bulls / strong bears smart traders

The institutional traders whose orders actually turn the market. Brooks constantly asks where they're entering: strong bulls buy pullbacks and the closes of bear bars in bull trends; strong bears sell rallies in bear trends. The goal is to trade with them, not against them.

02

Reading a single bar

Brooks reads charts one bar at a time. Every bar is a battle between buyers and sellers, and the close tells you who won.

trend bar

A bar with a meaningful body — the close moved clearly away from the open, so one side won the bar. Big bodies with small tails show strength. A series of them in one direction is the signature of a spike.

doji

A bar with a tiny or nonexistent body: neither bulls nor bears controlled it, making it a one-bar trading range. Brooks lumps all non-trend bars in here and considers naming subtypes (hammer, harami, etc.) a waste of energy — what matters is only trend bar vs. not.

reversal bar

A bar that opens moving one way and closes strongly the other way — for a bull reversal bar: open near the low, tail below, close near the high. It shows one side failed mid-bar. A good reversal bar is the minimum ticket for any countertrend trade.

signal bar

The bar that sets up your trade. It only becomes a signal bar if the next bar actually triggers the entry by trading beyond it. A strong signal bar (good body, closing in your direction, sensible context) is something Brooks insists on for every countertrend trade and prefers for every trade.

entry bar

The bar during which your order fills — normally on a stop one tick beyond the signal bar. A strong entry bar (a good trend bar in your direction) is early evidence the trade is working.

follow-through follow-through bar

One or more bars extending a move after the initial breakout or entry. Strong follow-through confirms the move; weak follow-through (a doji or opposite-color bar right after a breakout) warns the move may fail.

inside bar ii, iii

A bar entirely within the range of the prior bar — the market is pausing. Two in a row is an "ii," three is an "iii"; these often act as breakout-mode setups where a move can go either way, and are decent signal bars when context favors one side.

outside bar oio, ioi

A bar whose high and low both exceed the prior bar's — a mini trading range showing confusion. Brooks warns they trap eager traders on both sides; combinations like outside-inside-outside (oio) or inside-outside-inside (ioi) are breakout-mode patterns.

pause bar

A bar that doesn't extend the current move — an inside bar or small doji within a trend. Usually just a one-bar flag, not a reversal.

shaved body

A bar with no tail on one or both ends (open or close at the extreme). It signals urgency — in a strong trend, a shaved close means one side was still in full control at the bell.

tail wick / shadow

The thin line beyond the body. Tails mark rejected prices: a long tail at the bottom of a bar means sellers pushed lower and buyers slammed it back up — cumulative buying pressure.

bar pullback

In an upswing, the first bar with a low below the prior bar's low (and mirror for downswings). This is the atom of Brooks' bar counting — it's what starts a High 1 or Low 1 count.

the close matters most

Where a bar closes is its final verdict. Brooks treats the close of each bar — especially breakout bars and follow-through bars — as the single most reliable piece of information on the chart.

03

Trends

A trend is a series of directional moves where reversal attempts keep failing. Brooks divides every trend into a strong spike phase and a weaker channel phase.

trend

A market making trending highs and lows — in a bull trend, higher highs and higher lows; in a bear trend, lower highs and lower lows. The practical definition: a market where you'd feel comfortable holding a with-trend position through pullbacks.

swing / swing high / swing low

A swing is one leg of movement lasting many bars. A swing high is a bar whose high is above its neighbors on both sides (a local peak); a swing low is the mirror. These points become support, resistance, and stop-run targets.

leg

Any smaller trend within a larger structure — a pullback is a leg, each push in a wedge is a leg. Brooks constantly counts legs, because moves tend to come in twos (see ABC correction) and threes (see wedge).

spike momentum / impulse move

A run of consecutive trend bars in one direction with little overlap — the strongest phase of a trend, usually starting at a breakout. During a spike, traders enter at the market, on tiny pullbacks, and at the closes of bars, expecting a measured move.

channel

The slower, two-sided phase after a spike: the trend keeps making new extremes but with overlapping bars, tails, and pullbacks. A channel in a bull trend is bounded by a trend line below and a trend channel line above. Channels perpetually look like they're about to reverse — and mostly don't.

Brooks: a channel is a "wide, weak trend," and countertrend scalping inside one is a beginner account-killer.

spike and channel

The most common trend shape: spike, then channel, and — key insight — the eventual break of the channel usually falls back to test the start of the channel. That's why Brooks marks where every channel begins: it's a magnet for later.

trend line

A line along the bottoms of a bull trend (or tops of a bear trend), connecting higher lows. It defines the trend; a strong break of it is the mandatory first step before you're allowed to even think about a reversal trade.

trend channel line

The line on the opposite side of the trend, along the highs of a bull move. Markets that overshoot a trend channel line and reverse back through it often mark a climax and at least a two-legged correction.

micro trend line

A steep, short trend line across just a few bars in a strong move. A breakout above a bear micro trend line that fails becomes a great with-trend Low 1 entry — and vice versa.

micro channel

An extremely tight channel where every bar's extreme touches the line — no pullbacks at all. It signals relentless one-sided pressure (often an institutional program). Don't fade it; wait for the first breakout and then trade the breakout pullback.

tight channel

A steep channel with only tiny pullbacks. Treat it like a spike: with-trend entries only, even when countertrend signal bars look pretty.

stairs broad channel trend

A trend advancing in overlapping steps — push, deep pullback, push — where each pullback re-enters the prior step's range. Two-sided but still trending; the third or fourth push often sets up a fade back toward the prior step.

moving average 20-bar EMA

The only indicator on a Brooks chart: a 20-bar exponential moving average. It's a magnet and a rough trend filter — in bull trends price mostly holds above it, and pullbacks to it are the classic buy zone.

moving average gap bar

A bar whose high is entirely below a falling EMA (or low entirely above a rising one). The first MA gap bar in a mature bear trend often sets up a rally attempt; a long series of them means the trend is very strong.

20 gap bars

When roughly 20+ consecutive bars stay gapped on one side of the EMA without touching it, the trend is unusually strong — and the eventual first touch of the EMA usually sets up a with-trend entry, not a reversal.

buying pressure / selling pressure

Cumulative evidence of who's quietly winning: bull bodies, tails under bars, and two-bar bull reversals are buying pressure even inside a bear move. Pressure builds before price turns, which is how experienced traders anticipate reversals early.

pressing

What strong institutional traders do in a strong trend: instead of waiting for pullbacks, they keep adding — buying above prior highs, at the closes of bull bars, and on every tiny dip. When you see a market being pressed (each attempt to pull back gets bought within a bar), treat it like a spike: with-trend entries at worse prices beat waiting for a perfect pullback that never comes.

climax

A move that has gone too far, too fast — often one or more unusually large trend bars after an extended run, or a trend channel line overshoot. A climax usually leads to two-sided trading or a two-legged correction, not necessarily an opposite trend. Never buy a High 1 right after a buy climax.

melt-up / meltdown

An accelerating trend without meaningful pullbacks (melt-up in bulls, meltdown in bears). Usually ends in a climax.

trend from the open

A day that trends from the very first bars and never gives a comfortable pullback entry. Brooks says these are the days you must not wait for perfect setups — enter early, even at worse prices, or you'll watch the biggest day of the month leave without you.

trending trading range day

A day that stair-steps: a trading range forms, breaks out, forms another range at the new level, and so on. Trade each range as a range, and the breakouts as breakouts; the earlier range often gets tested later.

trend resumption day

A day with a morning trend, a long sideways lunch that convinces everyone the trend is over, then a resumption to a new extreme in the afternoon.

signs of strength in a trend

Brooks' checklist for how healthy a trend is: consecutive trend bars, closes near the extremes, small tails, gaps past prior swing points, shallow pullbacks that last only a bar or two, and pullbacks that don't reach the EMA. The more boxes ticked, the more you should only trade with-trend.

04

Pullbacks & flags

A pullback is a countertrend move that fails to reverse the trend — and to Brooks, every pullback is a flag: a small trading range that usually breaks out with the trend. This is where his bread-and-butter trades live.

pullback

A temporary move against the trend that stays "in bounds" — the trend's structure survives. Every pullback is simultaneously a small trend in the opposite direction and a small trading range. When it ends, the with-trend traders who were waiting pile back in.

bull flag / bear flag

Any pause or pullback in a trend, however it's shaped — sideways drift, two-legged dip, wedge, double bottom. A bull flag usually breaks out upward; a bear flag downward. Because 80% of reversal attempts fail, Brooks says every attempted top in a bull trend is more accurately called a bull flag.

high 1, 2, 3, 4 bar counting, buy side bread & butter

Counting the legs of a bull-trend pullback. The first bar whose high pokes above the prior bar's high is the High 1; if that bounce fails and the market dips again, the next upward poke is the High 2 (a two-legged pullback), and so on to High 3 (usually a wedge) and High 4. A High 2 at the EMA in a bull trend, with a decent signal bar, is one of the most reliable trades in the books (~60%).

Context rules: only buy a High 1 in the spike phase of a strong trend — never after a buy climax, and never near the top of a trading range, where the same pattern is a trap.

low 1, 2, 3, 4 bar counting, sell side bread & butter

The mirror image in bear trends: the first bar whose low pokes below the prior bar's low during a rally is the Low 1, the second attempt is the Low 2. A Low 2 at the EMA in a bear trend is the classic short. If a Low 4 fails, the bears have lost control.

ABC correction

The classic two-legged pullback labeled by its turns: leg A down, bounce B, leg C down (in a bull trend). Structurally the same thing as a High 2 — Brooks uses the terms interchangeably.

wedge flag three-push pullback

A pullback made of three pushes against the trend — Brooks calls any three-push pattern a wedge, whether or not the lines converge. A wedge bull flag (three pushes down in a bull trend) is a high-quality buy: three failed attempts tend to exhaust the sellers. Equivalent to a High 3.

double bottom bull flag

A pullback in a bull trend that makes two dips to about the same price before turning back up. It doesn't need to be at the day's low, and "about the same" is close enough. The mirror is the double top bear flag in bear trends.

double top pullback / double bottom pullback

A double top followed by a deep pullback that forms a lower high — a sell setup (mirror for the buy side). The deep pullback traps hopeful trend traders before the real move.

breakout pullback

The first small pullback (one to about five bars) shortly after a breakout. If the breakout was strong, this is often the best, safest entry of the whole move — you join the new trend with a defined stop after it has already proven itself.

breakout test

A pullback that returns close to the original breakout/entry price — often exactly where earlier entrants set breakeven stops. The market loves to touch that level, run the stops, and then go. If the test holds, it confirms the breakout.

final flag

The last flag of a mature trend — often a tight range or wedge — whose breakout quickly fails and reverses. Trends frequently end this way: the market breaks out of the flag one last time, traps the late entrants, and reverses back through it. The reversal often ends at the price of the final flag.

first pullback

The first pause after a strong move — the sequence typically runs: first pullback at a prior bar, then at a micro trend line, then the EMA, then an MA gap, then the major trend line. Each deeper pullback level marks weakening momentum.

nesting

A small pattern forming inside a larger one of the same kind — e.g., a small double top nested at the second peak of a bigger double top. Nested patterns trigger entries on both scales, adding fuel.

05

Trading ranges

A trading range is two-sided balance: bulls buy the bottom, bears sell the top, and 80% of breakout attempts fail. The rule up here is buy low, sell high, expect scalps — never buy strength near the top or sell weakness near the bottom.

trading range

Sideways, two-sided price action — any time two or more bars largely overlap, a range is forming. Every range is a pullback on a higher timeframe. Uncertainty is its hallmark: if you can't tell the always-in direction, you're in a range.

tight trading range

A range of heavily overlapping small bars where neither side can move price. Brooks' rule: tight trading ranges trump everything — stop entries lose here no matter how good the setup looks. Wait for the breakout to resolve.

barbwire barb wire avoid

His name for the nastiest tight range: three or more largely overlapping bars, at least one a doji, often with big tails. It shreds traders who enter on stops in either direction. Stand aside, or (experienced only) fade small breakouts of it with limit orders.

ledge

A small range where several bars share an identical high (or low), forming a flat edge. The breakout beyond a ledge often goes just a little and reverses.

triangle

A converging range — to Brooks, just another trading range with narrowing swings (and any five-legged sideways pattern qualifies). Expect breakout mode: be ready to trade either the breakout or its failure.

expanding triangle

The opposite: each swing gets bigger, sweeping stops on both sides. The fourth or fifth push often reverses the whole pattern — a reversal setup at the extremes.

midday the lunchtime range caution

The middle hours of the session (roughly 11:30–1:30 ET in US index futures) lean heavily toward tight ranges and barbwire: volume drops, stop entries stop working, and good-looking setups fail both ways. Brooks' pattern of the day: the best trades cluster in the first two hours and the final hour; through the middle, trade less, expect scalps only, or stay flat.

breakout mode

A spot where a breakout in either direction is plausible and should have follow-through — e.g., a tight triangle late in the day, or an ii pattern after a big move. Don't predict; prepare orders both ways or wait.

magnet

Any price that reliably attracts the market: prior swing highs/lows, the EMA, gaps, measured move targets, the start of a channel, old signal bars, round numbers, yesterday's close. Trends often stall or reverse exactly at magnets, so Brooks always knows which magnets sit above and below.

measured move

A projection equal to the height of a prior structure: a spike of X points projects roughly another X from the breakout; a range of height H projects H beyond its edge. Measured moves are where institutions take profits — natural targets for you too.

buy vacuum / sell vacuum

When everyone expects a magnet just ahead to get tested, the strong traders on the other side simply stop trading until price gets there — so the market gets "sucked" quickly to the level in a deceptively strong-looking spike. A sell vacuum below support often ends with a sharp reversal up. Never chase a spike into a magnet.

leg 1 = leg 2 counting legs

Corrections and swings tend toward symmetry: the second leg of a move often roughly equals the first. Another everyday measured-move tool.

06

Breakouts

Every trend bar is a breakout of something, and every breakout is a fight: one side betting on follow-through, the other betting on failure. The skill is judging which side wins — before the next bar closes.

breakout

Any move beyond a prior price of significance: a swing high or low, a trend line, a range boundary, even just the prior bar's high. Successful breakouts show strength (big bodies, small tails, follow-through); most breakout attempts from ranges fail.

breakout bar

The (usually strong) trend bar that creates the breakout. Its size and close are the first vote on whether the breakout is real — a big body closing near the extreme is serious; a big bar closing mid-range is suspect.

signs of strength in a breakout

Brooks' checklist for a breakout likely to succeed: large trend body closing near its extreme, follow-through on the next bar, small or no tails, the move gapping past magnets, and no immediate strong opposite bar. The more signs, the more you treat pullbacks as entries rather than failure warnings.

failed breakout failure, false breakout

A breakout that reverses before reaching even a scalper's profit, trapping the traders who chased it. Failed breakouts at the edges of ranges are among the most dependable fade setups — you enter where the trapped traders are forced out.

failed failure breakout pullback in disguise

A failure that itself fails: the breakout stalls, "reverses," but within a few bars resumes in the original direction. Since it's effectively a second signal, it's unusually reliable. Patterns fail, and failures fail — the second resolution is usually the real one.

five-tick failure Emini-specific

In the E-mini, a trade that runs exactly five ticks past the signal bar and stalls — one tick short of filling most scalpers' one-point profit orders — then reverses. A tell that the move trapped scalpers, and often a setup in the opposite direction.

gap

Any space between bars — the open beyond yesterday's range (opening gap), a bar's low above the EMA (moving average gap), or a breakout leaving prior prices behind. Brooks treats gaps like spikes: they either get filled (failure) or act as measuring points (strength).

micro measuring gap

When a strong breakout bar's extreme doesn't overlap the bar before the prior bar, that little gap marks momentum — its midpoint often becomes the middle of the whole move, giving a measured-move projection.

gap reversal

An opening gap that fails: the market opens beyond yesterday's range and immediately reverses back through it. One of the classic open-of-day trades.

opening range

The high and low of roughly the first 30–60 minutes of the day. It commonly ends up being a third to a half of the day's final range, so a breakout of it projects a measured move that often marks the day's extreme. Breakouts of the opening range — and especially their failures — set the tone for the whole session.

opening reversal

A sharp reversal in the first 15–90 minutes, typically at a magnet (yesterday's high/low/close, the opening range extreme). Brooks rates strong opening reversals among the best swing trades of the day, often setting the day's high or low.

trap

Any move that lures traders in just before going the other way — or scares them out just before going their way. Brooks reads every chart looking for who is trapped, because trapped traders' forced exits are free fuel for your trade.

trapped in / trapped out

Trapped in a losing position (your stop is about to be run) or trapped out of a winner (a stop run took you out just before the move). A sudden stop run that immediately resumes the trend is itself a reliable with-trend entry.

early longs / early shorts

Traders who enter while the signal bar is still forming instead of waiting for the stop trigger. They get better prices when right — and become the trapped fuel when the bar closes against them.

07

Reversals

Most reversal attempts fail — so Brooks demands a strict sequence before any countertrend trade: a strong trend line break first, then a test of the old extreme, then a strong signal bar. No sequence, no trade.

trend reversal reversal

A change from bull trend to bear trend or vice versa. In practice most "reversals" become trading ranges rather than opposite trends — but the entry is traded the same way, and even a range usually pays the swing entry.

major trend reversal sequence required

The only reversal Brooks lets beginners consider, and only in this order: (1) a strong break of a significant trend line, proving the countertrend side has real power; (2) a pullback that tests the old trend extreme — a higher low, double bottom, or lower low at a bottom (mirror at a top); (3) a strong reversal signal bar at that test. Then the trade targets at least a two-legged move.

trend line break

Price closing convincingly beyond the trend line — the first evidence the countertrend side is strong enough to matter. After the break, the market almost always returns to test the old extreme; only that test can set up the real reversal trade.

test

A return to a prior significant price — an old high or low, a breakout point, an entry price — to see if it holds. "The market tests everything" is core Brooks: most reversals are tests that failed to break through.

higher low / lower high

The two best reversal entries: after a bear trend line break, buying the pullback that bottoms above the old low (higher low); after a bull trend line break, shorting the rally that peaks below the old high (lower high). Lower lows and higher highs also work when they reverse quickly — the stop run makes them stronger.

double top / double bottom

Two pushes to about the same price that both fail. Anywhere on the chart, not just at day extremes — and remember: in a trend, the pattern is more often a flag than a reversal. It earns reversal status only with the trend line break sequence behind it.

micro double top / bottom

The two-bar version: consecutive (or near-consecutive) bars with matching highs (or lows). Common as final flags and as the small reversals that start bigger ones.

head and shoulders

Three pushes where the middle one is the extreme. Brooks is dismissive of the textbook mystique: to him it's just a triangle / trading range, and like all "reversal patterns" it more often becomes a continuation flag. Trade it only as part of the major-reversal sequence.

wedge top / wedge bottom

Three pushes to new extremes, typically overshooting the trend channel line on the third — the market tried three times and failed. A wedge top after an extended bull trend, with a strong bear signal bar, is one of the few countertrend setups Brooks respects, targeting at least two legs down.

climactic reversal spike-and-opposite-spike, V reversal

An unusually large trend bar (or several) after an extended move, answered immediately by an equally strong spike the other way. Rare but violent; usually best to wait for the pullback after the opposite spike rather than chase it.

expanding triangle reversal

The fourth or fifth swing of an expanding triangle poking beyond the prior extreme and reversing — a stop-run reversal with a built-in target back across the pattern.

countertrend

Any trade against the always-in direction. For most traders, a losing strategy: risk is as large as reward and probability is low. Brooks' shorthand: a countertrend trade without a prior trend line break is a donation.

countertrend scalp avoid

Trying to catch a small pullback against a trend "because it's due." Beginners lose on most of these — channels manufacture beautiful-looking countertrend signal bars all day, and almost all of them fail.

fade

To trade against a move you expect to fail — e.g., shorting a breakout above a trading range. Fading range extremes is proper range trading; fading trends is how accounts die. Same word, opposite outcomes, all context.

08

Orders, risk & trade management

Brooks is precise about mechanics: how you enter, where the stop lives, and whether you're scalping or swinging — decided before entry, not during the panic.

stop entry

Entering with a stop order beyond a bar — buying one tick above a signal bar's high, shorting one tick below its low. The market must already move your way to fill you. This is the default Brooks entry, and the only style he recommends for beginners.

limit entry

Entering on a limit order against the current move — e.g., buying at or below the prior bar's low in a strong bull trend, where institutions buy. Requires reading the chart well enough to buy while price falls toward you; experienced traders only.

protective stop

The order that takes you out when you're wrong — initially one tick beyond the signal bar, or beyond the spike for swing trades. Brooks: always have one in the market, because the greatest danger isn't the market, it's you.

money stop

A stop based on a fixed dollar or point amount (say, two points in the E-mini) rather than chart structure. Fine when the structural stop is close; when the right stop is far away, trade smaller instead of tightening the stop.

breakeven stop

Moving your protective stop to your entry price once the trade has moved well in your favor. Expect the market to test it (the breakout test); if you're stopped out and the setup is still valid, you re-enter.

trailing stop

Ratcheting your stop behind the trend — below each new higher low in a bull swing. Brooks trails loosely; tight trails hand your position to the first noise pullback.

scalp

A trade held for a small, quick profit — around a point or two in the E-mini. The catch: scalping needs a very high win rate to survive the math, which beginners don't have. His blunt advice: beginners should not scalp.

scalper's profit

The standard small target (e.g., four ticks in the E-mini, which usually requires a six-tick move so your limit gets filled). Watching whether scalpers get paid tells you who's in control bar to bar.

swing

A trade held for a larger move through pullbacks, with reward at least twice the risk. Lower win rate, bigger wins — the correct style for beginners, who should swing part of every good entry.

scratch

Exiting a trade at roughly breakeven because the premise faded. A scratch is a win in disguise: it's the discipline that keeps small mistakes small.

scaling in / scaling out

Adding to a position as it goes against you at better prices (scaling in — experienced traders only, with a hard final stop), or taking partial profits as it goes your way (scaling out — how most traders manage swings: take some at 1–2× risk, let the rest run).

with trend

Any trade in the direction of the always-in position. The default. When in doubt, with-trend or flat.

flat

Holding no position. A legitimate stance Brooks praises constantly: you don't have to trade, and waiting through unclear price action is itself an edge.

day trade / long / short / tick / pip / lot

Housekeeping terms: a day trade exits the same day; long profits from rises, short from falls; a tick is the smallest price increment (a quarter point in the E-mini), a pip its forex cousin; a lot is one unit of whatever you trade (one contract, 100 shares).

blown account

An account drained below margin requirements — the end of the road. Brooks' recurring warning: it's caused less by bad setups than by oversized positions and skipped stops.

position sizing "I don't care" size

Trade small enough that a loss doesn't rattle you and that you can take every valid signal. His test: if you're skipping decent setups or agonizing over each tick, you're trading too big — cut size until the fear leaves, because your emotions are your opponent's edge.

09

Trader's wisdom

Not chart patterns — mindset patterns. These lines from the trading-guidelines chapter get quoted by Brooks students more than any setup.

price is truth

Never argue with the chart. The market leads the news, the fundamentals, and your opinion; if you think it should go up but it prints a strong sell setup, take the sell. Trade the trade you have, not the one you want.

"I don't care"

The working mantra: I don't care about this one trade's outcome, about the TV experts, about the other timeframes, about missing moves. Only the one chart in front of me and my rules. Achieved through small position size, not willpower.

talk yourself out of bad trades

Brooks says this is the single most valuable activity of the trading day. The money you don't lose on low-quality trades is the profit margin of everything else.

too early is worse than too late

Since most reversals and breakouts fail, an early entry usually fails with them, while a late entry into an established trend is usually still fine — trends go further than anyone expects.

close is close enough

Everything on a chart is in a gray fog and perfection is rare. If something closely resembles a reliable pattern, expect it to trade like that pattern. Precision-hunting is procrastination.

if you're certain, don't take the trade

When a setup feels like a sure thing, you're missing what the equally smart trader on the other side sees — and you'll size up and misjudge the odds. Confidence should peak around 60%, never 100%.

those who talk don't know

His verdict on financial TV, chat rooms, and gurus: entertainment that costs you money. No saviors — do your own analysis off the one chart, or don't trade.

wait

If you've lost touch with the chart — you were checking email and suddenly a giant bar prints — do nothing. Decisions made while out of sync lose money on average, even when a few work.

one chart, no indicators

His prescription for beginners: a single 5-minute chart of one market, a 20 EMA, stop entries on with-trend pullbacks, reward at least equal to risk, tiny size. Adding charts and indicators multiplies reasons to skip good trades and take bad ones.

trading is a job that takes years

Brooks took over ten years to become consistently profitable, and he compares the books to a manual for violin: understanding it and performing it are separate, equally hard skills. Expect the same and plan (and size) accordingly.
